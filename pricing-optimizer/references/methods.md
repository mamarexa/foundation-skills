# Pricing methods

## Price–volume trade-off

The contribution margin (CM) is the share of the price left after variable costs:
CM = (price − variable cost per unit) ÷ price.

- **Price rise of p (as a fraction):** profit stays the same if volume falls by no more than
  p ÷ (CM + p). For example, +20% on a 60% CM tolerates a 25% volume loss.
- **Price cut of p:** volume must rise by at least p ÷ (CM − p) just to stand still. For
  example, −10% on a 30% CM needs +50% volume. Price cuts on thin margins rarely pay.
- **Price elasticity** = % change in volume ÷ % change in price. Most small businesses have no
  measured elasticity. Say that, and bracket the volume assumption with a downside case instead
  of pretending to precision.

Use these only to *set up* options. Report outcomes from `compute_feasibility`.

## Value-based pricing (economic value to the customer)

1. Pick one customer segment. The value differs by segment, so do each separately.
2. **Reference value:** the price of the customer's next-best alternative, which could be a
   competitor, doing it themselves, or doing nothing.
3. **What makes this offer different:** list each difference and its money value to the
   customer: time saved × their cost of time, revenue gained, cost avoided, risk reduced.
   Negative differences count too.
4. The economic value to the customer is the reference value plus the sum of the differences.
   Price somewhere between the reference value and that total, leaving the customer a clear
   share of the difference as their reason to switch. How much you can capture depends on how
   easily they can switch and how strong the competition is. Say which way you leaned and why.

## Willingness-to-pay research (when the founder can reach customers)

**Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter.** Four questions, asked in this order:

1. At what price would this be so expensive you wouldn't consider buying it? (too expensive)
2. At what price would it start to seem expensive, but you'd still consider it? (expensive)
3. At what price would it seem a bargain, a great buy for the money? (cheap)
4. At what price would it be so cheap you'd doubt its quality? (too cheap)

Plot the cumulative answers. The acceptable range runs from where the "too cheap" and
"expensive" curves cross up to where the "cheap" and "too expensive" curves cross. With under
about 30 respondents, treat it as directional only.

**Gabor-Granger.** Show one price at a time: "Would you buy at X?" Step the price up after a
yes and down after a no. The answers form a demand curve, and price × purchase likelihood
shows the revenue-maximising point. Use it once you have a rough range, for example from Van
Westendorp.

**Conjoint** is for packaging questions: trade-offs between features and price. It needs
specialist tools and a larger sample. Recommend it only for decisions where it's worth that.

Rules: ask real prospects, not friends, and never make up or simulate survey results. Stated
willingness to pay usually overstates what people actually pay. Real commitments such as
pre-orders, deposits or letters of intent are stronger evidence.

## Pocket price waterfall (discount leakage)

List price
− on-invoice discounts (promo, volume, negotiated)
− off-invoice items (rebates, free shipping, extended payment terms, free extras, payment fees)
= **pocket price**: what the business actually keeps per sale.

In McKinsey's studies, off-invoice leakage averaged about 16% of list price. For a small
business, check discounting habits, free delivery, "friends" pricing, payment-processing fees
and late payment. Tightening these is often the cheapest price increase available.
